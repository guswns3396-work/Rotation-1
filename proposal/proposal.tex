\section{INTRODUCTION and AIMs of the Study}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

including what is known and unknown, and requires a detailed literature
search. This should contain the following four elements, and will be used
to construct the Introduction and Discussion sections of the manuscript:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define the problem or research question.
  \item Describe what is known about the research relative to what you plan
        to study. For example, what studies have already examined an aspect
        of this research question, and what remaining questions exist that
        this research project will address.
  \item What are the gaps in knowledge that this study will address
        (usually 2--3).
  \item Describe the hypotheses of the study questions.
\end{enumerate}

% [importance of depression] [higher rates among interns]
Depression is among the leading contributors to the global burden of disease
\cite{whiteford_global_2013, noauthor_global_2022} and carries a substantial
economic cost \cite{greenberg_economic_2015}. Chronic occupational stress is a
well-documented contributor to depressive disorders \cite{tennant_work-related_2001},
and physicians in training are particularly exposed. Depression and depressive
symptoms affect roughly one in four medical students
\cite{rotenstein_prevalence_2016, dyrbye_systematic_2006}, and a pooled 28.8\%
of resident physicians, with a median absolute increase in depressive symptoms
of 15.8\% at the onset of residency training \cite{mata_prevalence_2015}.
Demanding and unpredictable schedules further limit interns' access to
traditional mental health services \cite{necamp_assessing_2020}.

% [potential of mobile health] [why JITAIs are useful]
Mobile health (mHealth) interventions offer a way to deliver support within daily
life. Text-message and handheld-device interventions have produced positive
behavior change across a range of health domains
\cite{fjeldsoe_behavior_2009, de_leon_characterizing_2014, patrick_text_2009,
franklin_randomized_2006, king_promoting_2008}, and smartphone-based mental health
interventions reduce depressive symptoms \cite{firth_efficacy_2017,
linardon_efficacy_2019}. Yet sustained engagement is difficult: treatment burden
and fatigue undermine adherence \cite{heckman_treatment_2015}, users frequently
disengage \cite{zhang_factors_2017}, and the timing and frequency of prompts shape
how they are received \cite{morrison_effect_2017}. Just-in-time adaptive
interventions (JITAIs) address these challenges by tailoring the type, timing,
and intensity of support to an individual's changing state
\cite{nahum-shani_just--time_2016, heron_ecological_2010, riley_health_2011,
wang_just---moment_2019}. Micro-randomized trials (MRTs), which repeatedly
randomize intervention delivery within each participant, provide the data needed
to determine when and for whom such components are effective
\cite{qian_micro-randomized_2021, boruvka_assessing_2017}.

% [overview of therapeutic strategies] [heterogeneity of their effects]
The content of mHealth messages can draw on several established therapeutic
strategies, including cognitive therapy \cite{beck_cognitive_2024,
linardon_efficacy_2019}, behavioral activation \cite{lewinsohn_behavioral_1974,
dimidjian_randomized_2006}, mindfulness \cite{teasdale_prevention_2000,
economides_improvements_2018}, motivational interviewing
\cite{rubak_motivational_2005, shingleton_technology-delivered_2016}, and
distanced self-talk \cite{kross_self-distancing_2017}. The benefits of these
strategies are not uniform across individuals. Behavioral activation
outperformed cognitive therapy among more severely depressed patients
\cite{dimidjian_randomized_2006}; mindfulness-based cognitive therapy prevented
relapse only among patients with three or more prior episodes
\cite{teasdale_prevention_2000}; and when delivered by smartphone, behavioral
activation was more effective for patients with higher initial severity while
mindfulness was more effective for those with lower severity
\cite{ly_behavioural_2014}. Among medical interns specifically, the effects of
push notifications differ across subgroups \cite{xu_uncovering_2023} and depend
on recent mood, activity, and sleep \cite{necamp_assessing_2020}.

% [introduce IHS] [prior work used WCLS, which averages effects]
The Intern Health Study (IHS) follows first-year residents throughout
internship and embeds MRTs of push notifications targeting mood, physical
activity, and sleep. Analyzing a 6-month MRT of 1{,}565 interns with the weighted
and centered least squares (WCLS) estimator \cite{boruvka_assessing_2017}, NeCamp
et al.\ found that notification effects were moderated by the previous week's
mood, step count, and sleep \cite{necamp_assessing_2020}. However, WCLS estimates
causal excursion effects averaged across participants and decision points,
conditional on a prespecified set of moderators. Heterogeneity that these
moderators do not capture is averaged away, so opposing individual responses can
cancel and mask meaningful variation in who benefits. Moreover, subsequent IHS
cohorts deliver messages based on distinct therapeutic strategies whose
individual-level efficacy has not been examined.

% [DR-WCLS as a method to explore heterogeneity]
The doubly robust WCLS (DR-WCLS) estimator \cite{shi_meta-learning_2025} is well
suited to this problem. DR-WCLS constructs a doubly robust pseudo-outcome at
each decision point, which, because randomization probabilities are known in an
MRT, provides an unbiased signal of the treatment effect at that decision.
Unlike WCLS, which yields only population-averaged effects, these pseudo-outcomes
can be aggregated within each participant to estimate individual-level effects,
allowing us to test directly whether responsiveness varies across interns.
Leveraging this, we (1) estimate the causal excursion effects of distinct
therapeutic notification strategies on proximal mood, sleep, and activity;
(2) test whether treatment responsiveness varies significantly across
participants; and (3) identify baseline characteristics that predict positive
versus negative responsiveness.

% [hypotheses]
We hypothesize that:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[H1.] Sending a notification improves the proximal outcome in its own
        domain (mood, sleep, or activity) relative to sending no notification
        \cite{necamp_assessing_2020}.
  \item[H2.] Effects on the proximal outcome differ by therapeutic strategy.
  \item[H3.] Treatment responsiveness varies across participants beyond what
        would be expected by chance under the randomization design.
  \item[H4.] Baseline depressive severity, measured by the PHQ-9, moderates the
        effect of strategy, with behavioral strategy relatively more effective
        at higher severity and mindfulness relatively more effective at lower
        severity \cite{dimidjian_randomized_2006, ly_behavioural_2014}.
  \item[H5.] Mindfulness messages are more effective for interns with a history
        of depression \cite{teasdale_prevention_2000}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{STATISTICAL ANALYSIS}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

potential consult with our analysis team.}

Univariate approach:

\vspace{1.5em}

Multivariable approach:

We evaluate overall treatment efficacy and individual response heterogeneity using a multi-stage causal inference framework.
We begin by applying Doubly Robust Weighted and Centered Least Squares to estimate the overall marginal causal excursion effect across all participants and decision points.
Next, using the per-decision-point doubly robust pseudo-outcomes generated by this procedure, we fit participant-specific regressions using time-varying contextual variables to estimate individual baseline treatment effects.
To establish whether these individual treatment effects are statistically significant overall across the population, we compare observed effect magnitudes against a randomization null distribution generated by permuting treatment assignments under the design probabilities.
We then inspect the distribution of estimated participant effects and fit Gaussian Mixture Models to evaluate response heterogeneity and identify distinct responder subgroups.
Finally, if significant heterogeneity is confirmed, we regress participant baseline characteristics against individual treatment effects to determine which baseline profiles predict positive versus negative intervention responsiveness.

All analyses will be conducted across the complete study sample as well as stratified by participant device type to evaluate potential hardware-level variability in treatment estimates.

\section{LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY (Optional)}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
A primary limitation of this study concerns the preprocessing and harmonization of intensive time-series data across heterogeneous commercial devices.
Because participants were not explicitly differentiated by device model, establishing uniform measurement baselines across hardware platforms proved challenging.
Consequently, cross-device data harmonization relied on ad-hoc alignment procedures, which may introduce calibration noise or unmeasured sensor variance.
On the other hand, device stratification will reduce the power of the analysis.
Furthermore, the sensitivity of causal estimates to specific time-series preprocessing choices remains a potential source of analytical variability.
